H5The “voluntary contribution”: 10,000 shekels a truck, and an 11-day halt of commercial goods into Gaza

13–25 Oct 2026
  • Both sides agree money was asked for. The fight is over one word: “voluntary.” A request from the ministry that licenses you is not a neutral request.
  • Two different arrests. Israel: a merchant's son detained. Gaza's union head: a trader under house arrest for refusing to sell at 25% cash — and Israel's coordinator reportedly made lifting it the condition for letting goods in.
  • “Resumed” on 19 October — on paper. Arabic reporting says the crossing was then shut for days more; trucks flowed again on 25 October, at about 130 a day against 160–170 before.
  • The bill went to the hungry. UN OCHA: potatoes up 72%, the food basket up 20% on average.

Israel's COGAT said on 14 October 2026 that Gaza merchants had stopped coordinating commercial imports after Hamas-run authorities demanded about 10,000 shekels (~$3,200) per truck and that about 30% of goods be sold for cash, enforced, it said, by threats and the detention of a merchant's son; Gaza's Government Media Office denied any tax or fee and called the money a “voluntary community initiative” for a national relief fund. The head of Gaza's private truckers union confirmed the Ministry of Economy had asked for the 10,000 shekels — its undersecretary called it a donation to pay civil servants — but said Israel, not the merchants, stopped the trucks; commercial goods were cut for about 11 days, food prices jumped, and no receipt, fee order or audit of the fund has been made public.

A “voluntary contribution” requested by the ministry that decides whether you can trade is a strange kind of voluntary. A “merchants' strike” at a crossing only Israel controls is a strange kind of strike. Gazans paid for both.
⚠ Two parties to the war tell this story, and each is weighed as a party: Israel's COGAT controls what enters Gaza and wants no money reaching Hamas; the Hamas-run government needs cash to pay its staff and wants Israel blamed for the shortages. Neither has published a document. The closest thing to independent evidence is Gaza's own truckers union head, and he confirms only that the 10,000 shekels was requested — not that it was compulsory, not the threats, and not Israel's account of who stopped the trucks. Same facts, different verbs

Every outlet had the same three facts — a demand, a denial, a halt. Who the sentence makes guilty depends on the verb:

  • Al Jazeera: “Israel halts commercial goods from entering Gaza; Hamas denies tax claims” — Israel acts.
  • AP: “Commercial goods are no longer entering Gaza, raising fears of worsening shortages” — nobody acts; the consequence leads.
  • Times of Israel: “Israel cites Gazan merchants angered by Hamas taxes” — Israel's claim, attributed; the denial's word “voluntary” becomes “taxes.”
  • Alhurra (US-government-funded): “Gaza Traders Halt Shipments, Citing Hamas Extortion Demands” — the traders act; “extortion” in the headline.
  • Jerusalem Post: “Gaza aid shipments resume as Hamas backs down from extortion threats” — these were commercial shipments, not aid; and that Hamas “backed down” rests on merchants' reports COGAT itself says are unverified.
  • Shehab (Hamas-aligned): the government denies fees and warns the claims are being used to justify cutting goods — Israel acts, Hamas is the victim of a smear.

None of these headlines is false. Each is a choice.

The bigger money: who else takes a cut

The 10,000 shekels is the visible fee. It sits inside a much larger trade in access:

  • Israel chooses who may import. Only a short list of Israel-approved traders can bring commercial goods in — the Shms trader says eight. A licence that scarce is worth money to whoever holds it, and leverage to whoever grants it. reported
  • “Coordination fees.” Gaza's Chamber of Commerce says traders paid more than $2.06 billion in “illegal coordination” charges from October 2023 to 26 July 2026 (Gaza Herald, Alhurra). It doesn't publish its method or name who collected the money; the figure is unaudited. reported
  • Mada Masr's “Kings of famine” (original; MEMO summary) describes a profiteering system run by companies close to the Israeli military and the Egyptian state, naming Ibrahim al-Organi's Sons of Sinai group and five Israel-approved Palestinian trading firms. We could not open the original; we rely on the summary. reported
  • Cash is a commodity. Israel restricts the entry of new banknotes; worn notes are refused in shops; electronic payment carries commissions. Whoever can demand cash gains a scarce asset. That is the Hamas government's stated reason for the cash rule — and Israel's stated reason to suspect it. reported

The power vacuum behind it

  • A year after the October 2025 ceasefire, nobody else runs Gaza. The Palestinian technocratic committee (NCAG), formed in January 2026, had not been allowed to enter as of mid-October; the international stabilisation force was not deployed. Hamas's ministries still administer whatever is administered. reported
  • Hamas needs cash. Its own undersecretary said the money was to pay civil servants. Paying police and clerks is also how an authority stays an authority.
  • Israel needs Hamas to have none. Every shekel that reaches Hamas is, in Israel's framing, a breach of the ceasefire's logic. Its tool is the permit: pay Hamas and you lose the right to import.
  • Merchants are caught between two licences. One authority controls the gate, the other controls the street. Either can end a trader's business.
  • The 600-truck promise was never met anyway. The ceasefire floor is 600 trucks a day; COGAT now says Gaza needs about 250; Sheheiber put actual entries near 300, two-thirds commercial. A fee dispute that halves the commercial share hits a supply line that was already short.

What we don't know

  • Whether paying was in practice optional. open No list of payers and refusers, and no account of what happened to refusers, has been published.
  • How many trucks paid, how much was collected, and where it went. open No receipt, signed fee order, or fund accounts.
  • Whether a merchant's son was detained (COGAT) or a trader put under house arrest (Shms), or both — and who they are. open
  • Whether the 30% cash rule (COGAT), the 25% figure (Shms) and the Chamber of Commerce measure (Gaza government) are the same rule. open
  • Who actually stopped the trucks: merchants refusing to coordinate, or Israel conditioning entry on the trader's release. open Both can be true at once.
  • Why the crossing stayed shut for days after Israel announced the resumption on 19 October. open
  • Whether the demand was really dropped, or just paused. open COGAT says the merchants' word is not verification; Gaza's side says there was never a demand to drop.

How to weigh it

  • Something was asked for. Gaza's own truckers union confirms it, and the Hamas-run government does not deny it — it renames it. That much is as solid as this story gets.
  • “Voluntary” is a claim, not a finding. Neither is “extortion.” Both come from parties to the war, and neither has shown a document.
  • The union head's confirmation is narrow. He backs the amount and the request, and rejects Israel's account of who stopped the trucks. Quoting him as proof of the whole Israeli story misuses him.
  • Keep the fee and the cash rule apart. A 30%-cash sales rule is not a 30% tax.
  • Count channels, not outlets. Most English coverage goes back to one COGAT letter and one interview with Sheheiber, repeated.
  • The people who paid are known. Whatever the truth of the fee, Gaza's shoppers paid it in prices, and its hungry paid it in empty markets.
