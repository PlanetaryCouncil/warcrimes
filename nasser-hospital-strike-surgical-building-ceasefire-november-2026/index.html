20Nasser Hospital, again: a strike on the surgical building during the ceasefire

12 Nov 2026, about 00:30 local time · Last updated 12 Nov 2026
  • Six killed, including a surgeon and a 13-year-old, says Gaza's health ministry; the army names one target, a Hamas brigade commander, and no civilian toll.
  • The army says it warned “on several occasions”; the hospital director says no warning came. Neither claim can be checked independently — Israel keeps foreign press out of Gaza.
  • The army's three posts: about 363,000 views. The Gaza media office head's post: 1,305. Roughly 280 to one.

Shortly after midnight on 12 November 2026, an Israeli strike hit the al-Yasin surgical building of Nasser Medical Complex in Khan Younis, the largest hospital in southern Gaza. Gaza's Hamas-run health ministry says six people were killed — medical staff, patients and companions, among them surgeon Dr Rizq Abu Shaira and a 13-year-old boy — and about 30 injured. The Israeli army says it struck Mohammed Abu Ismail, commander of Hamas's Khan Younis Brigade, and other fighters using the hospital's second floor as a headquarters, after repeated warnings; the hospital's director says no warning was received, and Hamas denies any military use. The army's three posts drew about 363,000 views on X that day; the Gaza media office head's, about 1,300.

Two accounts of one strike, and they barely touch. Israel names a commander, draws his room on an illustration, and lists the hostages it says he held; Gaza names a surgeon and a schoolboy. Both may be true at once: that is exactly the case the law of war was written for, and why it demands warnings that are heeded or ignored on the record, and a strike whose cost is weighed. On a timeline, only one of them gets heard.
⚠ The army's “hospitals used by Hamas” map and its second-floor diagram are both captioned “Illustration”; the weapons photos on the map come from earlier raids, not this strike. The army has said “further updates on its results will follow”: as of 12 November it had not said whether Abu Ismail was killed. The view counts are X's own figures, read the evening of 12 November; the posts and Telegram statements are archived here.

What later reporting added

Later reporting, after the first day's posts, filled in some of those gaps without settling the rest. No public independent evidence of Hamas military use of the hospital that night was published; AP, AFP and DW record that the army's claim was made without accompanying proof, and that the named target, Mohammed Abu Ismail, was not on the published lists of the dead. The six deaths were confirmed by the hospital, Civil Defence, WHO and MSF, not only by Gaza's health ministry.

The hostages claim

The army says “dozens of hostages” were held at Nasser and that Abu Ismail held several of them, naming Nimrod Cohen, Matan Zangauker, Edan Alexander, Avera Mengistu, Col. Asaf Hamami, Staff Sgt. Oz Daniel and Capt. Omer Neutra. It gives no evidence in the posts. There is earlier testimony that fits part of it: Sharon Alony Cunio, freed in November 2023, told CNN and AP that she and her daughters spent most of their captivity in Nasser, in rooms holding 10 to 12 captives (AP via PBS). Hamas denied it then. reported

Holding hostages in a hospital in 2023 is a grave allegation in its own right. It doesn't establish what the second floor was used for in November 2026, which is what the strike was justified by.

What the army showed

  • A satellite view of the complex: “where senior Hamas terrorist and additional terrorists were hiding.” Shows the building, not who was in it.
  • A second-floor diagram marking the rooms of Abu Ismail, Abu Hosna (Tel al-Sultan Battalion) and Muhammad Barim (Rafah Brigade), captioned “Illustration”.
  • A map, “Hospitals in the Gaza Strip used by Hamas for terror purposes”, with weapons photos from Nasser, the European Hospital, al-Shifa, al-Quds and Kamal Adwan — earlier raids, also captioned “Illustration”.
  • A 23-second video and an audio clip it says is a warning call. We haven't been able to check either.

None of this is evidence about the night of the strike. It is the case for it.

What the law says

Hospitals are protected. They lose that protection only if used, outside their humanitarian function, to commit acts harmful to the enemy — and even then only after a warning, with a reasonable time limit where appropriate, has gone unheeded (ICRC, customary rule 28). The attack must still be proportionate: the expected harm to patients and staff weighed against the military advantage. So the questions here are the ones in the table: was the second floor used militarily, was a warning given and ignored, and was killing a surgeon and patients in a working surgical building proportionate to the target. None is answered by either side's posts.

Even if he was there: why then, why there?

Grant the army's account in full — the commander was in that room. The law still asks more than “was he there?”:

  • Every feasible precaution in the choice of means and methods of attack, to avoid or at least minimise harm to civilians (ICRC rule 17), and constant care to spare them (rule 15). Timing is a method. So is waiting.
  • Proportionality: no attack where the expected civilian harm is excessive against the concrete military advantage (rule 14).

Which leaves questions only the army can answer:

  • Why 00:30? The surgeon who survived says staff had just finished work and were going to sleep in the same building. A strike at night finds the most people in their beds.
  • Why the surgical building? Patients after surgery and in intensive care can't run, whatever the warning. A warning to evacuate a hospital floor means something different when the people on it are on drips.
  • Why not wait? The army says it used aerial surveillance before the strike. If it could see him, could it have struck him outside, on the road, at another hour? It hasn't said why it couldn't.
  • Why two missiles? The hospital says one didn't explode. What was the munition, and what blast was it expected to have inside a ward?

The army's likely answers, to be fair to them: commanders who live in hospitals rarely leave them; a window may not come twice; a precision munition aimed at one room is the precaution. Those are arguments, and they may hold. But they're for the army to make on the record, with evidence, and for an independent body to test — not for a timeline to assume. open

How to verify what really happened

Neither side's posts settle it. These would, and most can be asked for today:

  1. The names. The full list of the six dead with ages and roles — staff, patient or companion — from the health ministry, checked against hospital records, funerals and families. One name is already confirmed by a colleague on the BBC; one by the education ministry.
  2. The commander. Was Mohammed Abu Ismail killed? Hamas sometimes confirms its commanders' deaths, often late; the army promised “further updates.” If he survived or wasn't there, the case collapses. If he died, it starts — it doesn't end.
  3. The warning. The army released audio it says is a warning to a senior health official. When was the call, to whom, what was said, how long before the strike? The official can be named and asked; the hospital director says nothing came.
  4. The munition. Agencies photographed what appears to be the unexploded missile. Its markings identify the weapon and its expected blast — which speaks directly to proportionality inside a ward.
  5. The room. The army's diagram marks three commanders' rooms on the second floor. Did the strike hit those rooms or the surgical wards and the medics' sleeping area BBC filmed? Footage can be geolocated against the army's own satellite image.
  6. The second floor before the strike. Staff, patients and visitors know whether a corridor was closed to civilians. Independent interviews — WHO, the ICRC, journalists — could establish it.
  7. An independent look. WHO and OCHA can visit; Israel can let foreign journalists in, which it doesn't; the army's legal review (its Fact-Finding Assessment Mechanism) can be asked whether it is examining the strike and to publish its finding.

We'll record answers here as they come, from either side. open

Nasser before

The hospital's director says this is the seventh time Nasser has been hit in the war. In August 2025, a double strike killed 22 people including journalists and rescuers — see our record of it. The WHO says it has documented 1,056 attacks on health care in Gaza in three years, with 1,010 deaths (via BBC).
